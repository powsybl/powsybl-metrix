# Algorithm description

## Introduction
As part of its activities, RTE needs a tool capable, for a given grid, of **simulating its operation hour by hour**
and of providing an **estimate of its operating cost, in normal conditions and facing various contingencies**.
This is, for example, necessary for the study of the development of the electrical grid.

METRIX includes both an active-power-only grid calculation (load flow) and a flow optimization model
(Optimal Power Flow or OPF).

A **load flow** computes the distribution of flows on the network elements of the grid according to the characteristics of these
network elements and to the grid injections (generation and load). The result of a load flow makes it possible to observe and assess the constraints present.
An **OPF**, for its part, can modify the injections/loads and the topology so that, after running a load flow, there is no
constraint on the network elements (or so that they are, at least, minimized).

Consequently, the objective of METRIX is to find the least-cost adjustment of the generation plan so as to have
no flow constraint either in N or on contingencies. METRIX makes it possible to measure the extra cost generated by the grid
on the initial generation plan and to value a grid reinforcement.
In its version 6, METRIX is integrated into the ImaGrid platform, from which it can be launched on thousands of variants.
These variants make it possible to model uncertainties on load, renewable generation, the availability
of generators, etc.

In order to obtain a problem that is easier and faster to solve, METRIX uses the **DC approximation** to
model the grid. However, this simplification does not make it possible to detect voltage constraints, and even
less the problems linked to the dynamics of events.

Whether through ImaGrid or in direct execution, a base case as well as an ancillary file describing all the variants
to be computed are sent as input to METRIX. The latter then applies each of the variants to the base case and
returns a result for each variant. The results are presented as time series in ImaGrid.
This document presents the electrical system modelling used by METRIX, the logic of the global algorithm,
the optimization problem it solves and the features available within METRIX.


## Objective

**The goal of METRIX is to provide an estimate of the hourly operating cost of a grid operating in an optimal
manner**, i.e. at the lowest possible cost.

Let us consider a given grid. Since the behaviour of a grid is random, the operating cost is random as well.
Let $X$ be the random variable of the operating cost of this grid. What we want to compute with METRIX is
a minimization of the expectation of this random variable:

$$
\begin{aligned}
min(\mathbb{E}(X))
\end{aligned}
$$

Since we do not have a law to determine this expectation, we will use a statistical approach
and approximate it as the mean of a large number of equiprobable realizations. We therefore need to determine the
operating cost of the grid over a large number of hours (which we will call variants). Denoting $n$ the number of
realizations:

$$
\begin{aligned}
min(\mathbb{E}(X)) \approx  min(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n}x_i)
\end{aligned}
$$

Moreover, to simplify the model and to parallelize the simulation of variants, it was decided to make them
independent of one another: the realization of the variable on one variant is totally independent of its
realization on another variant. Consequently, minimizing the sum of the costs amounts to minimizing each hourly cost:


$$
\begin{aligned}
min(\mathbb{E}(X)) \approx  min(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n}x_i) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n}min(x_i)
\end{aligned}
$$

To estimate the minimum of the expectation of $X$, we will therefore have to minimize the operating cost of the grid
for each variant, and this over a large number of variants. This amounts to simulating, for each variant, an
optimal operation of the grid. To do so, we will use **Operations Research** tools.
Furthermore, to have different variants, we will need different situations in the grid:
in terms of generation costs, unavailable generators, loads of the consumption centres, etc. These
grid parameters will be represented by random variables, each variant having its own realization. METRIX
therefore solves a **stochastic optimization** problem.

## Problem statement
To simulate the real and optimal operation of the grid on a variant, we will break the problem down into two
optimization problems, solved one after the other.

### First problem
First, a generation–load balancing problem (***Adequacy phase***), simulating the
fact that, following a change in load, electricity producers adapt their generation to meet
the new demand, while ensuring a minimal operating cost. If the generation capacity is insufficient,
load shedding is also possible. The electrical grid is not taken into account in this phase; we
assume that all generating and consuming elements are located on the same copper plate without resistance.
By solving this problem, we obtain a **generation – load equality**.

Since **all the variables of the problem are continuous**, we use **Linear Programming**. To keep a statistical
approach, the choice of generators (for equal costs) is made **randomly**. However, to avoid
having a different solution at each resolution, this random choice is identical for a given
computing environment.

This step is illustrated in [the diagram below](#algo-adequacy-fig): the initial situation is unbalanced between demand
(150 MW) and generation (30 + 20 MW). The generators therefore increase their generation, calling first the cheapest
generation.

### Second problem
But since the power lines have not been taken into account, this **new balance may cause overloads
on them**. In a second phase (***Redispatching phase***), the grid must therefore be considered in its
entirety and **preventive actions** must be chosen to avoid overloads while ensuring
generation – load balance. These preventive actions group together RTE's means of action on the grid, namely the
**modification of the phase shifts of Phase Tap Changers and of the flows on HVDC Lines**. We also have
the possibility of modifying generation or load. However, these actions involve stakeholders
outside RTE, so their use will be penalized by a higher cost in the simulation.

In addition, contingencies may occur on the grid and disturb its operation (loss of a generator,
of a line, etc.); **curative actions** must therefore be chosen to avoid further overloads. These
actions are the same as in preventive, with, in addition, **topological remedial actions**: these open or
close lines or couplers of the grid, so as to modify its topology, and thus to modify the flows on the
lines.

Solving this second optimization problem will therefore make it possible to simulate the choice of the preventive and
curative actions guaranteeing the proper operation of the grid at a minimal cost. Since some curative actions are of the
“switch” type, **boolean variables** appear in the problem: we thus solve a **Mixed Integer Linear
Problem**.

The [diagram below](#algo-redis_fig) shows the preventive part of this second problem: after the grid is balanced, the
threshold constraints of the lines are added, which causes the overload of a line. So that its threshold is not
exceeded, we modify the generation of the generators while maintaining the supply – demand balance, which results in an
extra cost of €200.

These are therefore the two problems that must be solved in order to simulate the operation of the grid over the desired duration.
However, <r>for greater speed and to remain within a statistical approach</r>, the choice was made **not to
link the solution of variant $t$ to the initial situation of variant $t+1$**. The results of each variant
are **independent** of the other variants. Consequently, each set of problems {*Adequacy phase*, *Redispatching
phase*} is independent from one variant to another, and each variant can be solved in parallel.

## Assumptions and simplifications of the problem

### Representation of lines and management of their constraints

To simulate the *Redispatching phase*, we solve an optimization problem including constraints on the
flows of the lines. However, a grid is made of a very large number of lines. Since **only a few of them
are worth monitoring** during a simulation, we indicate as simulation input which are **the
lines whose flow must be constrained** and which are **the lines that can be left unmonitored**.
This reduces the amount of computation to be done without reducing the usefulness of the simulation.

Moreover, thanks to the structure of the problem, **we can avoid adding all the flow constraints
on the lines from the start**. Indeed, in an electrical grid, if lines are overloaded, this is generally due
to an overload on a small group of lines which then pass the excess on to other lines. By preventing overloads
on this original group of lines, we thus also eliminate the overloads on the other lines. **The
optimization problem can thus be simplified by reducing the number of constraints** to be taken into account, without
changing the validity of the simulation.

To solve the optimization problem, we will **proceed by micro-iterations**: at each micro-iteration, we
solve the problem; then we check whether, in the solution found, there are overloaded lines. Among these
lines, we look for which subgroup of lines generates overloads elsewhere, and therefore which subgroup of
lines must be constrained in order to make all the overloads disappear. These constraints are then added to the
problem, then we restart the resolution. And so on, until no more overloads
appear.

###	Preventive and curative actions to satisfy threshold constraints

To satisfy the grid constraints during the *Redispatching phase*, we use preventive actions (to
modify the grid before contingencies occur, so that they do not cause failures)
and curative actions (to correct the failures that occurred and keep satisfying the load).

Among these actions are the modification of generator generation and load shedding, just as
in the *Adequacy phase*. But there is also the use of **Phase Tap Changers** (PSTs), **HVDC
Lines** (LCCs) and **Topological Remedial Actions**.

PSTs are used to modify the phase on the lines on which they are installed, and thus to change the ratio
$\frac{\text{active power}}{\text{reactive power}}$. In other words, they make it possible to adjust the “power”
flowing on the lines, without touching generation or load.

The name of LCCs is fairly explicit: these lines do not carry alternating current. As with
PSTs, we can adjust the power flowing through them. They thus make it possible to transfer an adjustable power
between two nodes, and notably between two synchronous areas (i.e. two sets of nodes with different alternating
characteristics).

Finally, topological remedial actions (usable only in curative) correspond to the opening or closing of
lines or couplers in the grid: they modify the topology of the grid. As a consequence, they modify the
distribution matrix used to compute the flows on the lines, and therefore modify these flows themselves. Since their
use requires a full study before integrating them as an action in a simulation, we
allow **at most one topological remedial action per contingency**.

### Active-power-only approximation

#### Assumptions
The above-mentioned means of action are therefore used to avoid or reduce overloads on the lines. But to detect them,
the flows on these lines must first be computed.

For that, we can use the usual physical modelling, representing an electrical line as an
inductance and a resistance in series (see [diagram below](#algo-indRes_fig)).

```{figure} img/indRes.png
:name: algo-indRes_fig
:alt: Inductance and resistance in series
:width: 600px
:align: center

Inductance and resistance in series
```

In this case, the flow from $i$ to $j$, denoted $T_{ij}$, is computed as follows:

$$
T_{ij} = \frac{V_iV_j}{Z_{ij}}sin(\theta_i - \theta_j + \gamma_{ij}) + \frac{V_i^2}{Z_{ij}}sin(\gamma_{ij}) \\
Z = R + i\omega L \\
\gamma_{ij} = arctan(\frac{R}{\omega L})
$$

This expression can be computed. But <r>in the context of linear programming, it is more appropriate to use
linear expressions. This can be obtained with a relatively good match using the
</r>**<r>active-power-only approximation</r>, in which we assume that the source imposes a constant
global phase shift, as well as a common voltage**.

The approximation relies on 3 simplifying assumptions:
1. Each link is treated as a pure reactance: the resistance is assumed to be zero. We also neglect the
conductances and susceptances of the lines.
2. The phase differences between neighbouring vertices are small; consequently, we consider that
$sin(\theta_{i} − \theta_{j}) \approx \theta_{i}- \theta_{j}$.
3. The voltage is assumed to be uniform across the whole grid at a fixed value $V_{ref}$.

$$
V_i = V_j = V_{ref} = \text{constante} \\
R <<< \omega L \Rightarrow \gamma_{ij} \approx 0 \text{ car } arctan(0) = 0\\
\theta_i \approx \theta_j
$$

This implies that the active power flow on a line between nodes $i$ and $j$ is written:
$$
T_{ij} \approx V_{ref}^{2}Y_{ij}(\theta_i - \theta_j)\text{ avec }Y_{ij} = \frac{1}{Z_{ij}}
$$

Moreover, at each node $i$ of the grid, the active injection is expressed as:
$$
P_{i} = V_{ref}^{2} \times \sum_{j \in \alpha(i)} Y_{ij} \times (\theta_i - \theta_j)
$$
with $alpha(ji)$ the set of neighbouring nodes of node $i$.
This approximation therefore makes it possible to obtain a **linear relationship between flows and injections**.

In METRIX, we reason in terms of powers and not angles; but we deduce the latter from the former
thanks to the PTDF coefficients in the DC approximation. We can then compute the flow on each
line from the generation of the generators.

In return, we have no information on the reactive flow of the lines, on voltage drops, on
“maximum transmissible power” problems, etc. Losses are not modelled on the lines: the flow
entering a line comes out entirely on the other side: $T_{ij} = -T_{ji}$.

#### Expression of flows for METRIX

The above equations can be written in matrix form:
$$
[p] = V_{ref}^{2} \times [A] \times [\theta]
$$

With:
 - $[p]$ the nodal active injection vector,
 - $[\theta]$ the vector of phases at each node,
 - $[A]$ the admittance matrix of the grid. It is square and symmetric and its size is the number of nodes in the grid:
 $$
 [A] =
    \begin{cases}
        \alpha_{ii} = \sum_{j \in \alpha(i)} Y_{ij}\\
        \alpha_{ij} =
            \begin{cases}
                0\text{ si }j \notin \alpha(i)\\
                -Y_{ij}\text{ sinon}
            \end{cases}
    \end{cases}
 $$

In METRIX, the control variables are injection data (generation, load shedding or PST and HVDC setpoints):
$$
[\theta] = \frac{1}{V_{ref}^{2}}[A]^{-1}\times[p]
$$

Now $T_{ij} = V_{ref}^{2}\times Y_{ij}\times (\theta_i - \theta_j)$, so $T_{ij} = [Y][A]^{-1}[p]$, with $[Y]$ a
row vector of the size of the nodes:
$$
[Y] =
\begin{cases}
    y_{ij} = Y_{ij}\\
    y_{ji} = -Y_{ij}\\
    0\text{ sinon}
\end{cases}
$$

It is possible to compute the row vector:
$$
[B] = [Y][A]^{-1}
$$

We can then express the flow in a line as a function of the injection variables (hence of all the
control variables):
$$
T_{ij} = [B]\times[p]
$$

METRIX uses this expression of the flow to write a constraint in the optimization problem.

#### Existence of equivalent solutions
It is important to note that, since simulating the *Adequacy phase* amounts to solving an optimization problem,
there may be **equivalent solutions**, i.e. solutions with the same optimal cost, but different
actions on the grid to reach the balance. This leads to different initial situations for the
*Redispatching phase*, and therefore different optimal solutions, with completely different optimal costs.
Likewise, for the same initial situation resulting from the *Adequacy phase*, the *Redispatching phase* may also
return equivalent solutions.

## Grid modelling
We will now present the modelling of the grid elements and of the optimization problem in METRIX.
To do so, we will focus on **a single variant**.

### Synchronous areas
All the grid elements (generators, loads, PSTs, LCCs) are linked to one or more nodes. Each node is
part of a single synchronous area, and each synchronous area contains several thousand of them. Let $ZC$ denote the set
of synchronous areas of the grid.

### Generators

The METRIX model is not intended for fine optimization of generation. Consequently, the modelling of generators
is simplified. As a static model, METRIX ignores the start-up dynamics of generators and some operating
constraints of the various generation means. METRIX only knows their variation bounds and their costs.
All generators are described in the same way regardless of their type.

In the *Adequacy* (i.e. balancing) and *Redispatching* phases, METRIX always takes into account the maximum power
(*Pmax*) of the generator.
However, in the balancing phase, METRIX does not take into account the minimum power (*Pmin*) if it is
positive. In other words, METRIX can therefore start a generator between 0 and Pmax. In practice, this happens at
most for the marginal generator (the last adjusted generator).

In the *Redispatching* phase the Pmin are taken into account and METRIX therefore cannot shut down a generator that is
started in the balancing phase.

It is possible to control the participation of each generator in each of the phases through the definition of costs.
The following can be associated with each generator:
- The upward balancing cost, which represents the generation cost per MW used in the balancing phase.
- The downward balancing cost is used in the balancing phase.
- The upward 'redispatching' cost corresponds to the cost of increasing, in preventive, the setpoint power of the
generator in the adjustment mechanism. This cost is also used in curative.
- The downward 'redispatching' cost corresponds to the cost of decreasing, in preventive, the setpoint power of the
generator in the adjustment mechanism. This cost is also used in curative.

For a given phase, an upward and a downward cost must always be defined.
The start-up cost of generators is not taken into account by METRIX.

See [Generation variables](#prod_var)

#### Sign convention
Let there be an upward cost $\Gamma^{+}$ and a downward cost $\Gamma^{-}$. If we increase the generation of the generator
by $P^{+}$, it will cost $\Gamma^{+} \times P^{+}$. If we decrease the generation by $P^{-}$ ($P^{-} \geq 0$), it will
cost $\Gamma^{-} \times P^{-}$.

With a positive cost, it “costs” to modify a generation. With a negative cost, it “pays” to modify
a generation.

As a consequence, if negative values are used for downward costs and the downward cost of a
generator is greater in absolute value than the upward cost of another generator, METRIX may modify the generation
cost solely to benefit from this “opportunity”, without this being motivated by a balancing or flow constraint.
The parameters `adequacyCostOffset` and `redispatchingCostOffset` make it possible to counter this
behaviour in each of the phases: the offset is added to the generator and load shedding costs of the phase, then
the result is bounded from below by `generatorMinCost` (0.5 by default). For the order between all the costs
to be preserved, the offset must be at least equal to the absolute value of the largest negative cost increased by
this bound.

If nothing is specified (i.e. no cost is defined), all the generators of the grid can take part in both phases at
zero cost.

As soon as at least one generator is configured, only the generators for which a cost is defined for a phase can
take part in that phase. The generation setpoint of the other generators cannot be modified.
If too few generators can act, the model may not be able to find a solution to the constraints and will
then return an error code 1 (e.g. evacuation constraint on a generator that cannot be modified).

### Loads

METRIX only uses active load since it relies on the DC approximation. Another
consequence of this approximation is that no losses are modelled on the lines. Losses are estimated
a posteriori (see [Losses computed a posteriori](#posteriori_losses)).
Loads must therefore be entered with losses included.

In order to solve flow constraints, METRIX has the possibility of shedding load.

This load shedding can take 3 forms:
- Load shedding in the balancing phase to respect $P = C$
- Preventive load shedding to respect flow constraints
- Curative load shedding to respect flow constraints

The load shedding level is defined by a threshold which corresponds to the percentage of the load that can be shed.
The cost of this load shedding can also be defined (otherwise the default value, `COUTDEFA`, is used). The cost
and the load shedding threshold are used both for load shedding in the balancing phase and for preventive load
shedding in the *Redispatching* phase. Curative load shedding uses another cost and another threshold.

By default, if nothing is specified, all loads can be shed at 100% in both phases. As soon as at
least one load is configured, only the configured loads can be shed.
If too few loads can be shed, the model may not be able to find a solution to the constraints and will
return an error code 1.
If we wish to model several load shedding/curtailment costs associated with different thresholds, additional
fictitious loads must be created on the same node and the active load distributed across these loads.

See [Load variables](#conso_var)

### Lines and transformers

#### Phase Tap Changers

##### Modelling
In METRIX, a PST is linked to a quadripole and serves as a power exchanger between the two nodes of said quadripole:
it draws power from one node to send it to another.

To properly understand how PSTs work, one must first understand that METRIX uses powers
to perform its computations, whereas PSTs work with angles, by modifying the phase shift of the electrical signal.
However, changing the phase shift amounts to changing the active power of said electrical signal, through a simple
multiplication:

$$
Puissance = angle \cdot \frac{\pi}{180} \cdot U^2 \cdot Y_{i,j}
$$

With $U^2$ and $Y_{i,j}$ the voltage and impedance values of the quadripole supporting the PST, which are parameters
in METRIX. Since we will be speaking of angles or powers to describe the operation of
PSTs, in order not to be confused, it suffices to remember that the two are proportional according to this formula.

For METRIX computations, the quadripole supporting the PST will be split into two quadripoles in series. Let us name
***quad*** the initial quadripole, with $(y, r)$ its impedance and its resistance. It links the nodes *Or* and *Dest*
(i.e. origin and destination). We then create a **fictitious node** *Nf* as well as a **fictitious quadripole**
***quadFictif***, going from node *Or* to node *Nf* and with characteristics $(y/0.1, 0)$. At the same time, we modify
*quad* so that it goes from node *Nf* to node *Dest*. We also modify its characteristics so that they
become $(y/0.9, r)*.

```{figure} img/quad.png
:name: quad_fig
:alt: Transformation of the PST from the data to the modelling in METRIX
:width: 600px
:align: center

Transformation of the PST from the data to the modelling in METRIX
```

The PST is carried by *quadFictif*, and therefore provides the phase shift. The real quad *quad* provides the reactance part.
The losses related to r are computed a posteriori, when the solution is displayed.

**N.B.**: PSTs are carried by lines. Since the latter are ignored in the *Adequacy phase*, PSTs play no
role in the *Adequacy phase*.

##### Definition of the min and max values of PSTs

In reality, the phase shift of the electrical signal is obtained by moving from one tap to another. Consequently, within
METRIX, each PST is associated with a list of taps of increasing phase shift as well as with two maximum bounds
corresponding to the maximum numbers of tap changes upward and downward: *lowran* and *uppran*.
These bounds are used as follows, with $X$ the tap number of the initial phase shift of the PST:
 - upward, the phase shift cannot exceed $X + uppran$
 - downward, the phase shift cannot go below $X - lowran$

In METRIX, the phase shift tap number corresponds to that of the tap minimizing the distance between the
phase shifts of the PST and the one associated with the tap.

See [PST variables](#td_var)

like traditional quadripoles. An LCC is described by:
- Two converters, each converter being located between an AC node and a DC node;
- and a line between the two DC nodes.

Thus, an LCC line links two AC nodes:

The sign convention for the power flowing through a converter station is as follows: when a
setpoint of $n$ MW is given on station 1, this amounts to transferring $n$ MW from 1 to 2, which is equivalent

An LCC can be controlled in different ways: in power or in AC emulation (see parameters `DCNDROOP` and
`DCDROOPK`). Moreover, for both these control types, the control can be imposed (the flow power is then

$P_0$ being the setpoint power on the line, it may or may not be modified depending on the nature of the selected control.

HVDC losses are computed a posteriori (see [Losses computed a posteriori](#posteriori_losses)).

##### Link between synchronous areas
Since LCCs serve as a link between two nodes and carry direct current, they can also be used as
interconnections between different synchronous areas (unlike classical quadripoles).

##### HVDC lines controlled in AC emulation

A line can be controlled in AC emulation (see parameters *`DCNDROOP`* and *`DCDROOPK`*). The flow of the HVDC link
is then $𝑃_0 + 𝑘(\theta_2 − \theta_1)$. METRIX then inserts a PST of impedance $1/𝑘$ between the two injections in order to
respect the previous constraint. The PST ensures that the value of the overall flow of the HVDC link is always
between $P_{min}$ and $P_{max}$. The value of $P_0$, for its part, can be fixed or optimized by METRIX
(see parameter *`DCREGPUI`*).

**Modelling of LCCs in AC emulation**
For an LCC in AC emulation, a fictitious quadripole (named *quad0*) is associated with it, with the same origin and
destination nodes. The resistance of this quadripole is zero and its admittance is determined by a parameter provided
individually for each LCC in AC emulation. We then associate with this quadripole a fictitious PST with
optimized angle control, which therefore causes the creation of a new fictitious node *Nf* and of a (doubly) fictitious
quadripole *quad1*. The diagram below summarizes this situation.

### Contingencies

The solution found by METRIX (generation plan and load shedding) must be robust to a list of contingencies given as
input. We distinguish line contingencies and generator contingencies.

See [Contingency variables](#inc_var)

#### Line contingencies

METRIX is able to simulate the loss of one or more line(s). It always uses the DC approximation
and therefore transfer coefficients to simulate these contingencies. Here, the flow on line $ik$ after
the contingency simulating the loss of line $mn$ is written:
$$
T_{ik}^{N-k} = T_{ik}^{N} + \rho_{ik}^{mn} \cdot T_{mn}^{N}
$$

To determine $\rho_{ik}^{mn}$ (transfer coefficient), METRIX uses the Woodbury formula.

Note that HVDC lines do not ensure connectivity between their origin and end nodes.
Consequently, contingencies simulating the loss of the feeder line of the HVDC line will be discarded
(they break connectivity). On the other hand, if the contingency is well defined on the DC line (i.e. on the line between the two
DC nodes), then the contingency simulating the loss of the HVDC line will indeed be simulated.

#### Generator contingencies

When generator contingencies are defined, METRIX distributes the generation lost during the contingency across
all the other available generators (even if they are not actually started).

The global frequency reserve of the grid is computed from the power lost by the most
dimensioning generator contingency. This yields the “regulation half-band” on each generator (which is 0 if there is no
generator contingency). Before starting the resolution, METRIX lowers the Pmax of each generator by its “regulation
half-band”.

To simulate generator contingencies, METRIX uses a sensitivity coefficient. For each contingency $i$, METRIX
computes the influence on each line $ik$, denoted $\rho_{ik}^{g}$, of the loss of 1 MW on the generator $g$ in contingency and
the takeover of this lost MW by all the other available generators in proportion to their Pmax.

METRIX then deduces the flow on line $ik$ after the loss of generator $g$:
$$
T_{ik}^{N-k} = T_{ik}^{N} + \rho_{ik}^{g} \cdot P_{g}^{N}
$$

with $P_{g}^{N}$ the power delivered in N by the generator that was tripped.

The same strategy is used to simulate the loss of a DC line; the only difference is that the variation of 1 MW
is compensated between the origin node and the end node of the DC line.

#### Combined contingencies

METRIX also handles contingencies composed of loss of generators and lines. Given that we are in
the context of the DC approximation, the equations are linear and the order in which the loss of
the generator or of the line occurs does not matter.

The flow on line $ik$ after loss of line $mn$ and of generator $g$ is:
$$
T_{ik}^{N-k} = T_{ik}^{N} + \rho_{ik}^{g} \cdot P_{g}^{N} + \rho_{ik}^{mn} \cdot (T_{mn}^{N} + \rho_{mn}^{g} + P_{g}^{N})
$$

This amounts to considering that the generator contingency occurs first, then that the line contingency and the impact
of the generator contingency on this line are properly simulated ($T_{mn}^{N} + \rho_{mn}^{g} + P_{g}^{N}$).

#### Contingencies breaking connectivity

By default, contingencies breaking connectivity are excluded from the computation. It is nevertheless possible to take them into
account via the parameter `INCNOCON`. In this case, for each contingency, METRIX reports in the outputs the volume
of generation and/or load lost during the contingency.

If a topological remedial action makes it possible to recover part of this power, this information is given for
the initial contingency and for the remedial action.

### Curative actions

In order to correct contingencies, the user can model curative actions (i.e. activatable once the contingency
has occurred) representing the actions taken by an operator or an automaton to bring the flow back within the
admissible threshold.

In METRIX, curative actions can be:
- modifications of the setpoints of generators, PSTs or HVDC lines;
- or curative load shedding.

The optimizer will choose whether it is useful to modify the setpoint of the curative elements following the contingency. It will combine
the actions if necessary and will make sure that the setpoint modifications obviously do not generate a new
constraint on another line of the grid.

To respect a constraint on a temporary threshold before activation of curative actions, METRIX will
necessarily use a preventive action. This preventive action may be complemented, if necessary, by a curative
action to respect the permanent threshold after the contingency.

If the curative functionality is used to model the operation of an automaton, it must be taken
into account that the modelling is optimistic: indeed, an automaton will act only with the aim of removing the constraint
on the line it monitors; moreover, it will act even if this generates a constraint elsewhere. METRIX can use
a lever to remove any constraint present on the grid and it ensures that it does not create
new overloads elsewhere.

### Curative topological actions

Topological remedial actions are curative actions handled differently from the other curative actions, because
having an impact on the topology, they modify the transfer and sensitivity coefficients. They also have a
strong impact on the solving time of the optimization.

For METRIX, a topological remedial action is always linked to a contingency and consists of the opening (or closing) of
additional lines or couplers. When a remedial action is selected by the solver, METRIX checks that this
remedial action does not generate constraints on the other monitored network elements of the grid.

For a given contingency, METRIX will always favour the remedial actions located at the beginning of the list. Thus, if none of the
remedial actions provided is really effective, METRIX will choose the “do nothing” remedial action which is automatically added
at the very beginning of the list.

METRIX can combine a topological remedial action with other curative actions, but cannot combine two topological
remedial actions. All the desired combinations must be explicitly provided in the list supplied as input to the
computation.

Remedial actions may have very similar effects, hard to tell apart, which complicates the resolution of the
problem. To counter these pathological cases, the parameter `PAREQUIV` makes it possible to hide the remedial actions whose effect
appears similar to that of another remedial action.

A topological remedial action selected by METRIX makes it possible to remove (or relieve) the flow constraints linked to a
contingency. However, if this remedial action is composed of several actions, there is no guarantee that it will be possible
to carry out all these actions in practice. Likewise, a remedial action may be used to reconnect a branch lost
because of a contingency, but given the simplifications of the METRIX model, it is by no means certain that this would
actually be possible in practice.

By default, a remedial action cannot worsen the connectivity break of a contingency (i.e. increase the number of
disconnected vertices), unless the parameter `PARNOCON` is used.

It is possible to restrict the action of a remedial action to the presence of a constraint on a specific network element. In
this case, the remedial action cannot be selected as long as the network element in question is not under constraint.

(posteriori_losses)=
### Losses computed a posteriori

At the end of the computation, METRIX computes the losses a posteriori.

See [Table R8](#io-table_r8) and [Table R8B](#io-table_r8b).

#### On AC lines

Given the active flows it has computed, it estimates the losses on the dipoles with the formula:
$pertes = R \times (\frac{T}{V})^2$ where $R$ is the resistance of the line and $T$ the active power flowing through
the quadripole.

**N.B.**: this is an estimate of the losses on the active part only; the reactive flow is assumed to be
zero.

#### On DC lines

As regards direct current links, METRIX finds a setpoint power $P$ applied to the rectifier.
The losses on direct current links are broken down into three parts: the losses in each of the two
converters and the losses in the cable. The detail of the computation is below ($red$=rectifier and $ond$=inverter):
 - **Losses in the converter station on the rectifier side**
 The losses are proportional to the power flowing through the station: $P$. METRIX uses the loss coefficient
per station. $PerteStationOr=coeffPerteOr \times P$. At the origin of the cable, there is therefore the power $P_{orCable}=(1−coeffPerteOr)\times P$.
 - **Losses in the cable**
 $pertesCable=R\times I_{DC}^{2}$ with $I_{DC}=\frac{(V_{DC}^{red}−V_{DC}^{ond})}{R}$,
$P_{orCable}=V_{DC}^{red} \times I_{DC}$ and $V_{DC}^{ond}=V_{DC}^{nom}$ (i.e. the voltage at the inverter is equal to the
nominal DC voltage). The value of $V_{DC}^{red}$ is found by solving a degree-2 polynomial in $V_{DC}^{red}$,
which makes it possible to determine the losses on the cable.
 - **Losses in the converter station on the inverter side**
 The losses are proportional to the power flowing through the station $P_{exCable}$. METRIX uses the
loss coefficient per station: $PerteStationOnduleur = coeffPerteOnduleur \times P_{exCable}$, with
$P_{exCable} = P - pertesCable$. This computation of losses in DC links is the same as the one used in Convergence.

### Marginal variations

Marginal variations provide information on the constraints that limit the solution.
See [Table R4](#io-table_r4) and [Table R4B](#io-table_r4b).

#### Marginal variations on AC lines

The marginal variation (MV) of an alternating current (AC) line indicates the gain on the cost function if the flow limit
on the AC line were 1 MW higher. If what limited the problem was:
- A flow in N, the MV gives the gain on the cost function if the N threshold of the line were 1 MW higher.
- A flow in N-1, the MV gives the gain on the cost function if the N-1 threshold of the line were 1 MW higher.
For an MV in N-1, it is possible to know which contingency led to this limiting constraint.

#### Marginal variations on HVDC lines

An MV on an HVDC line indicates the gain on the cost function if the capacity of the HVDC link were 1 MW higher.
If the HVDC link can act in curative, several marginal variations will be given:
- The global HVDC MV indicates the gain on the cost function if the capacity of the HVDC link were 1 MW
higher. The gain is then detailed in the following MVs.
- The preventive MV corresponds to the gain on the cost function if the admissible range for the preventive setpoint
were increased by 1 MW.
- The curative MV per contingency i corresponds to the gain on the cost function if the admissible range for the
curative setpoint on contingency i were increased by 1 MW.

**N.B.**: today, in Assess, there is in practice only one admissible range for the HVDC setpoint. This
range is used by the HVDC lines in preventive as well as in curative. The last 2 MVs nevertheless provide
additional information: the global MV announces a gain when the admissible range is increased by 1 MW. Thanks
to the preventive and curative MVs, we can know whether this additional available MW would be used in preventive
or in curative and on which contingency.

**Note**: the MVs of an HVDC line are directly linked to the bound of the HVDC line (i.e. admissible range). Care must therefore be
taken in the interpretation if the HVDC bounds are taken from the variants: from one variant
to another, the gain announced by the MV does not correspond to the same additional MW (to the same operating range).

#### Marginal variations on loads

If the adjustment mechanism is not simulated, the MV is given for all loads. The load MV
indicates the impact on the cost function if the load were 1 MW lower.

Conversely, if the adjustment mechanism is simulated, the load MV is only returned on the nodes for
which the failure is non-zero. For the other nodes, METRIX is not able to assess the gain.

#### Marginal variations on monitored sections

The MV of a monitored section indicates the gain on the cost function if the upper bound of the sum of the flows
on the section were increased by 1 MW.

### Limitations

#### Limited domain of validity

Non-zero MVs point to a set of lines or security constraints that have a direct impact on the
function to be minimized: these are the first constraints to solve to lower the redispatching cost.
The MV value gives a gain on the cost function for a threshold change of 1 MW; however, it is not
guaranteed that this MV holds when the variation is no longer marginal (i.e. a variation of many MW).
In other words, increasing the N threshold of a line by $x$ MW may not bring a gain on the cost function
of $x$ times that of the MV.

Let us take the example of a radial branch made of two lines, the first having a lower IST than the second. The MV
will be associated with the first line. However, if we increase the IMAP of this first line, the second may
become limiting and the gain will thus be smaller than what the MV announced. This same phenomenon can occur
with generators that reach their Pmin or Pmax limit.

#### All other things being equal

The gain expressed by the MV must be understood “all other things being equal”, i.e. this gain corresponds
to the gain if only the capacity of the line is increased. Now, generally, such a change in the field implies
a modification of the reactance of the line as well. This would therefore change the result.

#### Hidden constraints

As described previously, the marginal variation indicates the gain on the cost function if the admissible range of the
line concerned were widened. However, this result does not take into account the limits of the other lines. As a
consequence, by relaxing the limit on one line, another line may become limiting for the problem.

METRIX has no way of making these so-called hidden constraints explicit in advance.

### Conclusions on MVs

MVs provide interesting information for the constraint analysis step, whether before reinforcement
or after reinforcement (residual constraints).

The value of MVs is to point out, among all the monitored lines and contingencies, the elements that are costly for the
objective function. Ranking these MVs makes it possible to classify the importance of these constraints.

## Micro-iterations

METRIX does not formulate all the security constraints from the outset: it starts from an unconstrained generation
plan and adds to the optimization problem only the constraints that are actually violated, iteration after
iteration. This section first describes this mechanism from a functional point of view, then its implementation.

### Functional description

This section describes how the micro-iteration loop works without reference to the code: security
criteria checked, available means of action and computation modes offered. The technical counterpart is described in
{ref}`micro-it-implementation`.

#### 1. General principle

METRIX seeks the **least-cost adjustment** of the generation plan so that no thermal limit
is violated, neither on the intact grid (N situation), nor after a credible contingency (N-k situations).

#### 2. Thermal thresholds and security regimes

Each monitored element (line, transformer or section) is assigned **thermal limits** that depend on the operating regime.
These limits represent the maximum active power flow that the element can withstand over different time horizons.

::::{container} diagram-card
```{raw} html
:file: img/micro_it_seuils.svg
```

:::{container} legend
The threshold used does not result from an increasing scale but from a decision tree with mutually exclusive cases, re-evaluated
at each comparison between a flow and a limit. The N threshold applies under normal operating
conditions. The post-contingency threshold corresponds to the stabilized regime, once the curative actions have been
applied; it is generally higher than the N threshold because it reflects the short-term thermal capacity
of the network element. The pre-curative-action threshold, more permissive still, covers the brief window between
the contingency and the execution of the curative action: it is only used on contingencies whose remedial actions have already been
activated. The fallback of the default case is not a maximum between two thresholds: when the ITAM option is active
and the post-contingency threshold is not provided, the pre-curative-action threshold is used in its place.

All these limits can be redefined per variant, which allows thermal calibrations
specific to a scenario.

#### 3. Security check: N and N-k scan

At each micro-iteration, after the optimizer has produced a generation dispatch, METRIX checks
that no monitored element exceeds its thermal limit. This check is performed in two steps.

::::{container} diagram-card
```{raw} html
:file: img/micro_it_scan_surete.svg
```

:::{container} legend
In the general case, post-contingency flows are not obtained by running a full load flow per
contingency. METRIX relies on linear sensitivity factors — transfer factors and
distribution factors — established from the Jacobian for a given topology, and reused from one micro-iteration to
the next as long as the topology does not change. The post-contingency flow is then expressed as a linear
combination of the pre-contingency flow and of the injection variations, which makes the N-k scan tractable even for
thousands of contingencies and hundreds of monitored elements.

One family of contingencies escapes this rule: those that break connectivity by isolating a pocket
still containing load or generation. Linearization no longer applies, and for these contingencies METRIX solves
a full load flow on the post-contingency topology at each micro-iteration. The corresponding
factorization is cached per topology, but the solve itself is redone at each
pass. The cost of the loop therefore grows with the number of such contingencies, which explains why they are
subject to a dedicated activation option.
:::
::::

---

#### 4. Curative means of action

When an overload is detected on a contingency, METRIX can mobilize two categories
of corrective actions, in addition to preventive redispatching.

::::{container} diagram-card
```{raw} html
:file: img/micro_it_actions_correctives.svg
```

:::{container} legend
For a given contingency, the optimizer must choose exactly one remedial action — the sum of the activation variables
is constrained to equal 1. This constraint never forces an action: as soon as a contingency
receives its first remedial action, METRIX automatically inserts at the head of the list a “do nothing” remedial action, which leaves the
topology intact. When the ITAM option is active, this fictitious remedial action is also created for
contingencies that have curative means but no declared topological remedial action. Curative actions
(generation, HVDC, phase shifters) are attached to the remedial actions and therefore operate in the post-remedial-action topology
selected; they are shared between the remedial actions of a given contingency, not duplicated. The activation cost
of a remedial action is proportional to the probability of the contingency and to the number of constraints already associated with
the contingency, so that remedial actions are only mobilized when preventive actions alone are
insufficient.
:::

##### ITAM mechanism

When the ITAM option is enabled (parameter `TESTITAM` in `fort.json`),
METRIX checks an additional threshold: the **pre-curative-action limit**
(`seuilMaxAvantCur_`), which represents the flow that the element can withstand
during the brief window between the contingency and the actual execution of the
curative action. This threshold is typically more permissive than the N-k threshold
(which applies in the stabilized regime after the curative action has been applied).

Enabling ITAM changes five points of the computation:

1. **Automatic threshold selection**: as long as no remedial action is activated
on a contingency, METRIX uses the classic N-k threshold (`seuilMaxInc_`). Once
the remedial actions are activated, the pre-curative threshold (`seuilMaxAvantCur_`)
applies for the subsequent detections on this contingency.

2. **Creation of “do nothing” remedial actions**: for contingencies that have
curative means available but no topological remedial action defined, METRIX
automatically creates a fictitious “do nothing” remedial action. This allows the
remedial action mechanism to kick in and then to be able to check the
pre-curative threshold.

3. **Re-detection on contingencies with activated remedial actions**: without ITAM, no
further constraints are detected on a contingency once its remedial actions have been
activated. With ITAM, detection continues in order to check that the
pre-curative threshold is respected; if it is not, new
constraints are added.

4. **Dedicated ITAM constraint**: upon the first activation of the remedial actions
of a contingency, METRIX adds an additional constraint to the LP
explicitly corresponding to the pre-curative threshold, in addition to the
per-remedial-action constraints.

5. **Recording of the pre-remedial-action threat**: for the outputs (R3
results notably), METRIX keeps track of the maximum threat encountered
before the activation of the remedial actions (`menaceMaxAvantParade_`), allowing
analysts to measure the level of transient overload.

Without ITAM (default case), only the stabilized N-k threshold is checked, and the
mechanism is neutralized on the five points above.

#### Functional additions

##### Constraint generation

Rather than formulating the complete security problem from the start (which would include one constraint
per monitored element per contingency — potentially millions of constraints), METRIX starts
with an unconstrained dispatch and progressively adds only the violated constraints.

##### Sensitivity factors for the fast N-k scan

Post-contingency flows are not computed by independent load flows. METRIX pre-computes
**power transfer distribution factors** (PTDF) and **line outage distribution factors** (LODF)
from the DC grid model. These linear sensitivity coefficients make it possible to estimate
post-contingency flows as a linear combination of pre-contingency flows and of
injection variations, making the N-k scan efficient in computation time.

##### Solver escalation

The optimization starts as a simple **Linear Program** (LP), solved by the Simplex method. As soon as
an integer variable is introduced, the problem becomes a **Mixed Integer Linear Program**
(MIP) and the solver switches to Branch & Bound. The three situations that trigger this switch are
detailed in {ref}`micro-it-choix-solveur`.

##### Loss re-estimation

After convergence, METRIX can optionally re-estimate the grid losses on the basis of the final distribution
of flows. If the gap between the re-estimated losses and the assumed losses exceeds the `SEUILPER` threshold (500 MW by
default), the whole computation is restarted with the updated loss rate. This loop is controlled by the `RELPERTE` parameter (disabled
by default). When it is enabled, it runs at most the configured number of times.

#### 1. Overview of a micro-iteration

Each micro-iteration follows a sequential pipeline: solving the LP, recomputing the phases (θ), detecting
the violated constraints, then adding these constraints to the problem. The loop stops when no constraint
is detected anymore, or when one of the stopping criteria is reached.

:::{container} legend
The solver is not called at iteration 1 nor when the problem contains no constraint (we start from
the economic stacking). The Simplex/MIP choice depends on the presence of integer variables (remedial actions,
AC emulation, curative counting). The loop stops when no constraint has been *written* into the
problem during the iteration — which is not strictly equivalent to “no constraint detected”:
constraints may have been detected and then all ignored, through equivalence between remedial actions or through the absence
of an action lever.
:::
::::

#### 2. Constraint detection — detailed logic

Detection is done in two phases: first in the N situation (intact grid), then in N-k for
each contingency. Four successive filters eliminate redundant or unsolvable constraints before retaining them.

::::{container} diagram-card
```{raw} html
:file: img/micro_it_detection.svg
```

:::{container} legend
**Semantics of “skip”:** a skip means “do not write this constraint into the LP” and move on to
the next element — the variant is not flagged as unsolvable. When a filter sets `ecarts_[icdt] = -1`
(“action possible” filters and N-k filter 2), the marker is permanent: the constraint is permanently
ignored and the possible overload will appear in the outputs as unresolved.

The filters marked *(WITHOUT_REDISPATCH / WITH_OVERLOAD)* only run in these two modes (slack
variables). The ITAM check blocks re-detection on contingencies whose remedial actions are already chosen, unless
the pre-curative threshold (ITAM) must be checked. After the inner loop, per contingency: if the contingency breaks
connectivity (pocket recoverable via a remedial action), no constraint was found, and the remedial actions
are not already activated, a fictitious constraint is added to force the contingency into the LP.

The final sort is not cosmetic: it establishes the priority order subsequently exploited by
the arbitration of `ajoutContraintes`. The type of a constraint depends on the situation evaluated, N or N-k, and on the
presence of an HVDC line in AC emulation in the monitored element — the latter being a property of the element,
a given element never mixes the two families. In practice, the sort therefore amounts, on a given element, to
placing its N constraint before its N-k constraints, and to ordering the latter by decreasing gap. This ranking
is what then allows the N constraint to take precedence, as the “masked by
N” filter already does upstream.

Finally, the two detection loops are bounded by the capacity of the `icdtQdt_` array: beyond it, detection
stops for this micro-iteration and resumes at the next one.
:::

#### 3. Adding constraints to the problem — detailed logic

Once the constraints are detected, they are translated into rows of the matrix of the optimization
problem. This is where the remedial actions are activated, where the integer and curative variables
are created, and where the flow cuts are written.

::::{container} diagram-card
```{raw} html
:file: img/micro_it_ajout_contraintes.svg
```

:::{container} legend
The 4 cases correspond to the if/else if/else if/else chain of the code. Case A: first constraint of a contingency
with remedial actions — creates the integer and curative variables, the activation constraints, and — if `TESTITAM` is
enabled and the pre-curative gap exceeds the detection threshold — a dedicated ITAM constraint on
`seuilMaxAvantCur_`. Case B: the constraint was detected on a remedial action (activated in a previous
micro-iteration) — creates the per-remedial-action constraints without an activation constraint. Case B': another constraint on a
contingency whose remedial actions were activated earlier in this same micro-iteration — creates the per-remedial-action
constraints with an activation constraint. Case C: plain N or N-k constraint, direct addition.

Case A follows the traversal of the contingency's remedial actions. A remedial action that disconnects all the network elements of the monitored
element cannot be under constraint on this element: it receives its variable and its activation
constraint, but no flow cut. The remaining remedial actions go through the pre-filtering heuristic,
which discards those whose post-action flow would remain close to the pre-action flow. Two optional
constraints are added along the way: a limitation of the number of curative actions when the remedial action offers
more than the configured maximum, and a valuation of the lost pocket when the remedial action isolates one containing
load or generation. After the loop come the topological choice constraint — which makes true the
statement “exactly one remedial action is activated” from the functional part — then, under ITAM, the constraint
on the pre-curative threshold.

Two location notes, useful for navigating the code: the integer activation variable of a
remedial action and the equivalence test between remedial action constraints are created in `ecrireCoupeTransit`, and the
curative variables of the contingency in `coeffPourQuadInc` — hence downstream of `ajoutContraintes`, not in the
body of case A.

#### Implementation additions

(micro-it-choix-solveur)=
##### Solver choice

The solver starts in simplex mode. It switches to MIP (Branch & Bound) as soon as an integer variable
is created. This happens when activating topological remedial actions (binary activation
variables), when modelling HVDC lines in AC emulation (fictitious PSTs — variables initially fixed
then unlocked if a constraint concerns them), and when creating counting variables for
curative actions if `NBMAXCUR` > 0 (=max number of curative actions per contingency). At micro-iteration 1, or
when the problem does not yet contain any constraint, the solver is not called: the result of the economic stacking
(off-grid phase) is used directly.

##### Arbitration of the constraints to add (`choixContraintesAajouter`)

This heuristic only runs if the number of detected constraints exceeds
`nb_max_contraints_by_iteration`, in `OPF` or `OPF_WITH_OVERLOAD` modes, and during micro-iterations 1 to 5.
When it runs, the number of constraints actually written into the problem for this micro-iteration
is additionally capped at `nb_max_contraints_by_iteration`; otherwise, all the detected constraints are written.

Two distinct filterings are combined. The constraints of a same monitored element are traversed in the order of the
sort, and a lower-ranked constraint is eclipsed (`ecrireContrainte_ = false`) if either of the two following
conditions is true.

**(a) Type priority.** It is not of the same type as the priority constraint. Since the sort places N constraints
before N-k ones, this amounts, on an element under constraint both in N and on contingency, to keeping only the
N constraint: we bet that correcting the N situation will suffice to resolve the post-contingency overload. The bet is
well founded, since the post-contingency flow is deduced from the N flow amplified by the transfer — acting on the N flow
thus acts on the N-k flow with a greater lever. It remains a bet: if the N-k constraint persists, it
will simply be re-detected at the next micro-iteration, `ecrireContrainte_` being reset to `true` at each
detection.

**(b) Near-redundancy.** The two constraints are of the same type, the contingency is not the N situation, it has no
curative elements, and the flows are within 1% of each other with thresholds less than 1 MW apart. This
covers the case of a same network element loaded in an almost identical way by several contingencies.

This heuristic extends the “masked by N” filter of the detection, which handles the certain case — an N-k
overload lower than the N overload on the same element. Here the bet is extended to the uncertain case, but only under
volume pressure. The final result does not depend on it, since the loop only stops when no more
constraints are detected; only the number of micro-iterations required may vary.

##### Fictitious pocket constraint

When a contingency breaks connectivity and the lost pocket is recoverable via a remedial action
(`pocheRecuperableEncuratif_`), no flow constraint has been detected on any
monitored element for this contingency, and the remedial actions have not already been activated in a
previous micro-iteration, a fictitious constraint is added to force the contingency into the
problem. Without it, the remedial action mechanism (Case A) would never trigger and the
solver would not know that it can “recover” the pocket.

##### Slack variables

In `OPF_WITHOUT_REDISPATCH` and `OPF_WITH_OVERLOAD` modes, each flow constraint can receive a
slack variable penalized in the objective function. This allows the solver to tolerate overloads
(at a high cost) rather than declare “no solution”. Remedial action activation constraints and
AC emulation constraints are excluded from this.

This is also what explains the asymmetry in the treatment of constraints without an action lever. When the flow
cut contains no non-zero coefficient — in other words when no variable of the problem influences the
flow concerned — the two slack-variable modes mark the constraint as permanently ignored and
continue the computation, whereas the `OPF` mode returns “no solution”. A user who observes a convergence
failure in `OPF` while the same case passes in `OPF_WITH_OVERLOAD` is most often running into this
difference.
